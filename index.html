01 / THE SHAPE OF THE MARKET

Four groups, and a gap in the middle

Put simply: the two rivals Stigg actually meets in deals, Metronome and Orb, lead on measuring usage after the fact. The usage counters only measure it. The billing suites, Zuora and Chargebee, are partners Stigg runs on top of, not competitors. Almost nobody sits where Stigg sits, a fast, independent layer that decides in the moment and leaves your billing where it is. One more rival is invisible on any tool map: the sharpest accounts in this market often try to build that layer themselves, and that homegrown instinct is the third competitor to answer.

The rivals that matter

Metronome, Orb

The two names Stigg actually goes head to head with in deals. Both are mature and both lead on measuring usage after the fact, and both were bought by payment companies, Metronome by Stripe and Orb by Adyen, so each also has a reason to steer you toward its owner's rails.

The billing systems you keep

Zuora, Chargebee, Stripe

Not rivals. Stigg runs on top of these and syncs both ways, and they are systems Stigg works alongside rather than competes with. They handle invoices and payments; Stigg adds the real-time control layer they leave you to build.

The usage counters

Lago, m3ter, Amberflo, OpenMeter

Newer tools that are very good at measuring usage and turning it into a bill. They count what happened. They do not stop it while it is happening.

The real-time control layer

Stigg, Schematic, Credyt

A small, new group that decides in the moment what a customer or an AI agent is allowed to do, and runs on top of the billing you already have. This is where Stigg sits. Only two others are nearby, Schematic and Credyt, and both are small and still maturing.

The third competitor is homegrown

The accounts worth winning here are the fastest, sharpest AI companies in the market, and they are also the most likely to build this layer in-house. So the real contest is often not against another vendor at all, it is against an engineering team that assumes it can build entitlements, credits, and real-time limits itself. That is answered in the message and the targeting, not with a feature list: the cost is not building it once, it is maintaining real-time enforcement and every edge case while the product roadmap waits.

Group one: the rivals that matter

Metronome

Usage billing, now part of Stripe

Measures product usage and turns it into invoices, popular with large usage-based software companies. Acquired by Stripe, closed January 2026. Was the best-funded independent option in this space.

What it means for Stigg: It bills at the end of the cycle, so nothing is stopped in the moment. Now that Stripe owns it, choosing Metronome leans a buyer toward Stripe's payment rails. That is the exact concern Stigg's independence answers.

Orb

Usage billing, now part of Adyen

Usage-based billing built for pricing changes, credits, and invoicing. A closed, hosted platform with quote-based pricing, now owned by the payments company Adyen.

What it means for Stigg: Same story as Metronome. It bills after the fact and now sits inside a payments company. Stigg stays independent and enforces before the cost is incurred.

Group two: the billing systems you keep

These are not competitors. Stigg runs on top of them and works alongside them, and they are shown here only to make clear what they leave for Stigg to do.

Zuora

Enterprise billing suite, a partner

The long-standing enterprise platform for subscriptions, billing, payments, and revenue reporting. Founded 2007, Redwood City. Taken private by Silver Lake and GIC for 1.7 billion dollars in early 2025, and recently absorbed a usage-pricing tool called Togai.

What it means for Stigg: A system Stigg runs on top of, not against. Zuora handles the billing; Stigg adds the real-time control layer it does not, without a migration.

Chargebee

Subscription and billing suite, a partner

A mature subscription-billing platform now adding usage and entitlement features as it moves up-market. Widely used, often described as a category unicorn.

What it means for Stigg: A full billing system Stigg layers on, not a control layer. It handles invoices; the in-product rules that decide what a customer can actually use are what Stigg adds on top.

Group three: the usage counters

Lago

Open-source usage billing

An open-source billing engine for usage, credits, and hybrid pricing, popular with teams that want full control. Free to self-host, with paid managed tiers.

What it means for Stigg: Strong on measuring usage and on control for teams that will run it themselves. It reconciles into an invoice; it does not enforce limits in real time inside the request. Stigg does, and offers a fully managed version.

m3ter

Metering and rating infrastructure

Measures usage and calculates bills, then feeds the numbers into your existing sales and finance systems. United Kingdom, raised roughly 14 million dollars.

What it means for Stigg: A back-office calculation layer for finance teams. It is about producing an accurate bill, not about stopping an AI agent mid-action. A different job than Stigg's.

Amberflo

Usage metering and AI monetization

Real-time usage metering for AI and cloud products, with credits, budgets, and cost controls. San Jose, founded by former AWS engineers, raised roughly 21 million dollars.

What it means for Stigg: The closest of the counters to Stigg on AI features. One thing to be straight about: Amberflo also offers AI credits and cost controls, so the AI point is a lead for Stigg, not a lock. Stigg's edge is enforcement in the request path plus running in your own cloud.

OpenMeter

Open-source metering, part of Kong

Open-source, real-time metering for AI and API usage, with some entitlement features. Now part of the API company Kong, available open-source or managed.

What it means for Stigg: Built for engineers who want to compose their own setup. Strong on metering, lighter on the packaged credits, governance, and enterprise support Stigg provides out of the box.

Group four: the real-time control layer, where Stigg sits

Schematic

Entitlements, built on Stripe

An entitlements layer that sits between your product and Stripe and enforces access in real time. Raised about 6.5 million dollars, launched a Stripe App.

What it means for Stigg: The nearest match to Stigg's message, almost word for word. The difference that matters: Schematic is built specifically for Stripe. Stigg works with Stripe, Zuora, Chargebee, and others, and can run in your own cloud. That breadth is the wedge.

Credyt

Real-time AI billing

Authorizes AI usage in real time before the cost is incurred, either as a full billing system or alongside your existing one. A newer, smaller entrant in the same camp as Stigg.

What it means for Stigg: Shares Stigg's core idea of deciding before spend. It is early and small, without Stigg's enterprise track record, named customers, or in-your-cloud option. A validator of the category more than a threat today.

03 / THE INVENTORY THAT MATTERS

Where the real value hides

Across five campaigns, a few pockets carry most of the value. These are the ones worth building pages and copy around, and the ones to watch in the search-term report.

The Stripe gap, highest intent

"Stripe metered billing" and its variants are a buyer describing Stigg's exact wedge: they have Stripe, are trying to make it enforce usage, and are finding out it does not do entitlements or credits. There is no persuasion step in that conversation, and it deserves its own page.

Entitlement management, owned outright

220 searches a month, effectively uncontested, and the only term set in the plan where Stigg is the literal answer rather than one option among several. Low cost, clean intent, worth protecting from the identity-and-access-management terms that share the word.

Metronome and Orb, not Zuora

Metronome and Orb are the direct rivals, same usage-runtime pitch and same buyer, and Stigg has a published comparison page for each. Zuora carries volume but most of it is navigational, and Stigg partners with Zuora rather than competing with it, so that ad group runs exact-match only as a light intercept. Expect Metronome and Orb to beat it on cost per lead.

The one page that must be built

Billing Platform Selection, 1,300 searches a month, is the largest ad group and the worst intent match: "subscription billing software" wants a biller, and Stigg sits on top of one. The fix is a single page that leads with "keep your biller, add the layer it is missing." It serves two campaigns, so it is the highest-return page build in the engagement.

The research-intent risk

The Usage-Based Pricing ad group may be founders researching how to price, not engineers sourcing infrastructure. It stays in because the same searcher becomes the buyer weeks later, but the rule is clear: if it converts below half the campaign rate after 100 clicks, pause it and let content own that demand.

The subscription category, watched daily

Campaign 4 gives the account the reach to spend at full budget by Month 3, but "billing software" and "billing system" sit right on top of the 200,000-plus invoicing searches. It lives or dies on the negative list, so the search-term report is read daily for the first week.

ATTRIBUTION / THE HUBSPOT ANSWER

See what the ads actually influenced

LinkedIn's own reporting cannot see the CRM, so it structurally cannot tell you whether the ads brought deals. Because Stigg runs HubSpot, this is where the measurement story lands. Connect HubSpot once, and deal and company data flows in and matches to the accounts that saw or engaged with the ads, with the window set to Stigg's real sales cycle.

Why this changes the read

Campaign Manager only sees clicks, so it often understates the truth. In one account, once conversions and CRM milestones were captured properly, the perceived cost per conversion fell from over $800 to about $12. The ads had not changed; the measurement had. That is the difference between optimizing for clicks and optimizing for pipeline.

What we report, and what we would not promise

Each layer has its own honest measure: reach and engaged accounts for Create, qualified reach and cost per qualified lead for Connect, search capture and landing-page conversion for Capture, and influenced pipeline and closed-won for the whole system. What we would not claim is full econometric marketing-mix modeling; that is a specialist discipline, usually run in-house or with a dedicated analytics partner. And every number rests on the conversion capture underneath it, which is why the events get defined and verified in week one, before anything spends.

Two clocks, read differently

The motion runs on two clocks. The warm ecosystem and in-market search convert fast, so their first weeks are read on booked demos and conversions. The named-account acquisition is slower, so its first sixty days are read on account penetration, persona reach, and repeat engagement across the list, the leading indicators that show the motion working while the logos form, not booked calls alone.
