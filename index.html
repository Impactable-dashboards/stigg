01 / THE SHAPE OF THE MARKET

Four groups, and a gap in the middle

The biggest competitor is an engineering team that plans to build this layer itself. Among vendors, Metronome (now part of Stripe) is the closest, with Orb, Chargebee, Schematic, and a newer entrant, Paid, moving into the space. Most of them measure usage after the fact and bill for it later. Zuora and Stripe are billing systems Stigg runs on top of, and partnerships with Zuora and Airwallex are in discussion. Almost nobody sits where Stigg sits: an independent layer that decides in the moment and leaves your billing where it is.

The rivals that matter

Metronome, Orb, Chargebee, Paid

The vendors Stigg meets in deals. Metronome and Orb are mature and lead on measuring usage after the fact, and both were bought by payment companies (Stripe and Adyen). Chargebee is moving up from subscription billing into usage and entitlements, and Paid is a newer entrant.

The billing systems you keep

Zuora, Stripe, Airwallex

Not rivals. Stigg runs on top of these and syncs both ways, and partnerships with Zuora and Airwallex are in discussion. They handle invoices and payments; Stigg adds the real-time control layer they leave you to build.

The usage counters

Lago, m3ter, Amberflo, OpenMeter

Newer tools that are very good at measuring usage and turning it into a bill. They count what happened. They do not stop it while it is happening.

The real-time control layer

Stigg, Schematic, Credyt

A small, new group that decides in the moment what a customer or an AI agent is allowed to do, and runs on top of the billing you already have. This is where Stigg sits. Only two others are nearby, Schematic and Credyt, and both are small and still maturing.

The biggest competitor is building in-house

Most deals start when an in-house build hits a wall: a new pricing model it cannot support, maintenance eating developer time, enterprise customers who cannot see their own AI usage, or agents running up costs. The cost is not building it once; it is maintaining real-time enforcement and every edge case while the roadmap waits.

Group two: the billing systems you keep

These are not competitors. Stigg runs on top of them and works alongside them, and they are shown here only to make clear what they leave for Stigg to do.

Zuora

Enterprise billing suite, a partner

The long-standing enterprise platform for subscriptions, billing, payments, and revenue reporting. Founded 2007, Redwood City. Taken private by Silver Lake and GIC for 1.7 billion dollars in early 2025, and recently absorbed a usage-pricing tool called Togai.

What it means for Stigg: A system Stigg runs on top of, not against. Zuora handles the billing; Stigg adds the real-time control layer it does not, without a migration.

Group three: the usage counters

Lago

Open-source usage billing

An open-source billing engine for usage, credits, and hybrid pricing, popular with teams that want full control. Free to self-host, with paid managed tiers.

What it means for Stigg: Strong on measuring usage and on control for teams that will run it themselves. It reconciles into an invoice; it does not enforce limits in real time inside the request. Stigg does, and offers a fully managed version.

m3ter

Metering and rating infrastructure

Measures usage and calculates bills, then feeds the numbers into sales and finance systems. Acquired by Salesforce in 2026, so it now sits inside a CRM suite.

What it means for Stigg: A back-office calculation layer for finance teams. It is about producing an accurate bill, not about stopping an AI agent mid-action. A different job than Stigg's.

Amberflo

Usage metering and AI monetization

Real-time usage metering for AI and cloud products, with credits, budgets, and cost controls. San Jose, founded by former AWS engineers, raised roughly 21 million dollars.

What it means for Stigg: The closest of the counters to Stigg on AI features. One thing to be straight about: Amberflo also offers AI credits and cost controls, so the AI point is a lead for Stigg, not a lock. Stigg's edge is enforcement in the request path plus running in your own cloud.

OpenMeter

Open-source metering, part of Kong

Open-source, real-time metering for AI and API usage, with some entitlement features. Now part of the API company Kong, available open-source or managed.

What it means for Stigg: Built for engineers who want to compose their own setup. Strong on metering, lighter on the packaged credits, governance, and enterprise support Stigg provides out of the box.

Group four: the real-time control layer, where Stigg sits

Schematic

Entitlements on top of Stripe
How they position: "Ship any pricing model": entitlements enforced at runtime, and pricing changed without code.
Who they sell to: Product and go-to-market teams at SaaS and AI companies that bill through Stripe.
How they sell: Through Stripe: Stripe chose Schematic for entitlements on Stripe Billing, with a native Stripe app launched at Stripe Sessions 2026.
Named customers: Plotly, Automox, Florence, Sema4.ai. About 12 million dollars raised; Boulder, Colorado.
Strong at: Fast pricing changes and feature access for Stripe users.
Where Stigg wins: Schematic is built around Stripe, and Stripe now also owns Metronome. Stigg is the independent option: it works with Stripe, Zuora, or any biller, runs in the customer's own cloud, and is built for credit ledgers at over a million events a second.
Sources: Schematic and Stripe · Crunchbase News

Credyt

Real-time wallets for AI builders
How they position: Check the wallet balance before the work runs, and debit it in real time.
Who they sell to: AI builders and smaller platforms, including teams setting up billing from inside their coding tools.
How they price: Free to start, then one dollar per active wallet a month, with Stripe for payments.
Named customers: None published. Launched November 2025, London.
Strong at: Fast setup for prepaid credit products.
Where Stigg wins: Credyt's message is the closest to Stigg's, so Stigg leads on depth: entitlements beyond wallets, the existing biller kept, deployment in the customer's own cloud, and enterprise compliance (SOC 2, ISO 9001).
Sources: Credyt · Crunchbase

05 / WHAT NOBODY ELSE IS SAYING

The open ground Stigg can own

Each of these is a position Stigg can hold on the strength of what it already does today. Each is written the way a customer would understand it.

It says no in the moment, before the money is spent

Strongest lane
What it means: Most tools here add up usage and send a bill at the end of the month. By then a single customer or AI agent may already have run up a large cost. Stigg checks whether an action is allowed and stops it right away, in a few thousandths of a second, before the cost happens.
How Stigg is different: This is Stigg's clearest strength. The counters and the billing suites reconcile after the fact. Only Schematic and Credyt sit near this. Metronome and Orb now offer spend caps and alerts, but they act on metered usage, not on the request, so the lane is mostly open rather than empty.
Who it is for: The finance leader watching AI costs climb, and the platform engineer who otherwise builds the guardrails by hand.
How big a deal: The strongest and most defensible position. It is the one thing the after-the-fact tools cannot match.

Keep the billing system you already run

Wide open, and newly so
What it means: You do not have to rip out and replace your current billing to get all of this. Stigg sits on top of Stripe, Zuora, Chargebee, and others, and syncs both ways. There is no migration project.
How Stigg is different: This matters more now that the two most popular middle options belong to payment companies. Stigg stays independent of any payment processor. Schematic makes a similar promise, but only for Stripe.
Who it is for: The engineering leader who dreads a billing migration, and the finance leader who does not want to be tied to one payment company.
How big a deal: Freshly opened by the Stripe and Adyen deals. A timely, ownable position.

Built for AI usage from day one, including limits on AI agents

Strong lead
What it means: Newer AI products do not just charge per seat. They spend real money on every request, token, and AI agent action. Stigg handles credits, budgets, and limits per user, per team, and per AI agent, out of the box.
How Stigg is different: The older suites were built for subscriptions. The counters measure AI usage but focus on the bill. Stigg's per-agent budget caps, enforced the moment an action happens, are a specific current strength. One thing to be straight about: Amberflo also plays here, so this is a lead, not a lock.
Who it is for: The AI product team launching credit-based or agent-based pricing.
How big a deal: A strong lead, best paired with the real-time point above.

Run it inside your own cloud, so your data never leaves

Open at the top end
What it means: For large or regulated companies, sending usage data to an outside vendor can be a dealbreaker. Stigg can run entirely inside the customer's own cloud, with no per-event bill.
How Stigg is different: Most competitors are hosted only. The main alternative is open-source tools you self-host, like Lago and OpenMeter, which your own team must run and maintain. Stigg offers a fully managed version inside your cloud, plus an air-gapped option and a path to government-grade compliance.
Who it is for: Enterprise and frontier AI teams with strict data rules.
How big a deal: Mostly open at the managed, in-your-cloud end, which is the part enterprises actually want.

Change your pricing without a coding project

Solid, shared
What it means: When pricing rules live inside your product's code, every plan change becomes an engineering ticket that takes weeks. Stigg keeps those rules outside the code, so a business team can change a plan or launch a tier the same day.
How Stigg is different: This is the original Stigg promise and it still holds. The billing suites handle invoices but leave the in-product rules for you to build. Schematic offers this too, so it is a position Stigg shares rather than owns alone.
Who it is for: The growth, product, or monetization leader who owns pricing but waits on engineering to change it.
How big a deal: Solid and real, but shared with Schematic. Use it as support, not as the headline.

Let enterprise customers see their own AI usage

Open, rising
What it means: When AI usage spikes, enterprise buyers want to see what they used, by whom, and what is left, before the invoice arrives. Stigg gives every end customer that visibility from auditable, financial-grade credit balances.
How Stigg is different: The after-the-fact tools report to the vendor at invoice time. Stigg shares control with the vendor's own customers, live.
Who it is for: Engineering leaders and AI engineers whose enterprise customers are asking for usage visibility and spend limits.
How big a deal: One of the most common buying triggers; it becomes urgent with every usage spike.

The real cost of building it yourself

Answers the top competitor
What it means: Building entitlements, credits, and limits takes a sprint. Maintaining real-time enforcement and the edge cases (overdrafts, expiry, burn-down order) takes the roadmap.
How Stigg is different: No vendor frames itself against the in-house build; they compare against each other. Stigg can name the competitor that actually shows up in most deals.
Who it is for: CTOs and founders weighing build versus buy, often under a strategic pricing deadline.
How big a deal: The highest-return message, because building in-house is the competitor in most deals.
What buyers have lived through

The AI pricing problems that made news in 2025 were not billing problems. 10 / KEY TAKEAWAYS

The short version

  • The biggest competitor is an engineering team building it in-house; most deals start when that build hits a wall.
  • Among vendors, the market split into groups, and Stigg sits nearly alone in the most valuable one: a real-time layer that runs on top of your billing.
  • The two most popular middle options were just bought by payment companies, which makes Stigg's independence from payment companies a timely point.

11 / ONE HONEST NOTE

The growth direction, not a current claim

Where the whole market, Stigg included, has not arrived yet

Turning all this usage data into a clear revenue picture a sales or finance team can act on is something nobody in this field has fully solved, and early reviews note Stigg's own reporting can feel rigid. So that is not a current advantage to advertise; it is the direction to build toward, and it needs a system around it rather than a single feature. The same is true of getting this story in front of the right buyers at scale. Both belong in the plan for how the work gets done, not in the list of things Stigg already owns.

ATTRIBUTION / THE HUBSPOT ANSWER

See what the ads actually influenced

LinkedIn's own reporting cannot see the CRM, so it structurally cannot tell you whether the ads brought deals. Because Stigg runs HubSpot, this is where measurement happens. Connect HubSpot once, and deal and company data flows in and matches to the accounts that saw or engaged with the ads, with the window set to Stigg's real sales cycle.

Why this changes the read

Campaign Manager only sees clicks, so it often understates the truth. In one account, once conversions and CRM milestones were captured properly, the perceived cost per conversion fell from over $800 to about $12. The ads had not changed; the measurement had. That is the difference between optimizing for clicks and optimizing for pipeline.

What we report, and what we would not promise

Each layer has its own honest measure: reach and engaged accounts for Create, qualified reach and cost per qualified lead for Connect, search capture and landing-page conversion for Capture, and influenced pipeline and closed-won for the whole system. What we would not claim is full econometric marketing-mix modeling; that is a specialist discipline, usually run in-house or with a dedicated analytics partner. And every number rests on the conversion capture underneath it, which is why the events get defined and verified in week one, before anything spends.

Two clocks, read differently

The motion runs on two clocks. The warm ecosystem and in-market search convert fast, so their first weeks are read on booked demos and conversions. The named-account acquisition is slower, so its first sixty days are read on account penetration, persona reach, and repeat engagement across the list, the leading indicators that show the motion working while the logos form, not booked calls alone.
